Featured Work#

Fowl Heat

Recipient of the Design NSF grant 2411742  ·  Play Fowl Heat on itch.io

As temperatures rise, animals worldwide are encountering intense, sometimes deadly, heat. Reimagined from the research of Dr. Liz Derryberry and Dr. Emmy James, play as an adult bird during the breeding season, working to maintain your own health and that of your chicks under intense, unrelenting heat.

Click to expand

Fowl Heat is an online and physical game developed under Cary Staples in the Unity Game Engine. As one of the two computer scientists on the team, I worked alongside Dr. Emmy James to adapt their research data from “Collaborative Research: Is behavior on the front-line of solving the problem of inescapable heat? Leveraging intraspecific variation in a cavity-nesting bird thriving in heat” into an educational game about birds surviving heat events through behaviors.

Physical Boardgame

What began as a paper prototype expanded into an additional way to play the game we were building. The core systems remained the same, with resource gathering, nest and risk management, and random events driving the main gameplay loop. Some changes benefit a physical game, like a simplification of one or more of these events, and the removal of obfuscation that forms a digital game’s architecture.

Gameplay Systems, Behaviors and Resources

The main player input driving player expression are cards that come in two forms, behaviors and resources.

Behavior cards are actions that a bird can take which reduce or increase a stat among the three meters: Hunger, Heat, and Risk.

These behaviors may have additional effects depending on the location the cards are played in. For example, the Bathe card reduces heat more when played on a River tile.

Resource cards are materials or consumables that a bird can use on itself, its nest, or its chicks. Some cards are rarer than others, such as white sticks, which reduce heat more than a normal stick. Some cards, like bugs, change behavior when played on nest tiles or other tiles.

The hand size of the player is limited to five in both the digital and physical version of the game. This drives a risk-reward system, as the survivability of the parent increases when behaviors fill the hand, but the lack of resources may bring danger to the chicks in an underdeveloped nest.

Gameplay Systems, Events

The event system drives the emergent gameplay most associated with roguelike games that we drew inspiration from.

Events occur each time a bird travels to a new tile and can be positive or negative. An example of a positive event could be an increased availability of Bug resources kickstarted by a lawnmower nearby, while a negative event is usually the arrival of a predator.

Events can affect the bird’s Hunger, Heat or Risk meter, as is the case with predators increasing the risk of the bird.

Events can also affect the bird’s hand, as is the case with resource events allowing the player to choose between two resources, or Friend events allowing the player to choose between two behavior cards to add to their hand.

The third type of event can affect a global state of either the entire game or a singular tile. One example being the previously mentioned lawnmower event, which increases the availability of bugs on the tile the event was encountered.

Gameplay Systems Design

Both gameplay systems discussed were built with encapsulation and templating in mind. In other words, it takes very little effort to add a new card, or a new event, and each system allows a developer to quickly and easily adjust the effects or outcomes of both events and cards occurring. For example, a new “Heatwave” event that increases the heat of all cards for a variable number of turns is not difficult to adapt into the systems in place. This is mostly achieved through the use of Unity Scriptable Objects and Prefabs.

On top of my work on the gameplay systems, my responsibilities included the creation of a code design doc that outlined how to design game objects for consistent logic across portions of the project outside of each team member’s scope, as well as the general user interface.
